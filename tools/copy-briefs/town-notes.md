# Banana Town — the letters the residents write to you

Banana World's post office works: you can write to a neighbour and they can write back. But at ten
players, most mailboxes are empty most of the time, and an empty mailbox is where a social feature
quietly dies. The plan is blunt about the fix (`docs/town-jobs-plan.md` §6):

> **The residents write too.** Nib, Moss, Bean and Stamp post to you: a thank-you after a good week,
> a note after a Curse Night. The mailbox is never dead, even on a Tuesday when nobody else is on.
> **This is the load-bearing beam, not a flourish.**

This job is those letters. Four residents, two occasions, and the whole point is that the mailbox
has something real in it on a quiet day.

## The two occasions

### `welcome` — the very first time somebody opens their mailbox
There has never been a letter in it. This is the first thing they will ever read in there, and it
has to do one job: **make the post office feel like a place where post happens.** A resident noticed
the new sign on the fence and wrote.

⭐ It may **not** be a tutorial. It does not explain the mailbox, does not tell them to write back,
does not mention the address book or a button or any part of the game. It is a neighbour saying
hello to somebody who has just moved in, and that is all.

### `quiet` — nothing has arrived for a good while
The box has been empty for days. A resident writes for no reason at all, which is the reason: people
who like you write when nothing is happening. It must never mention that the box was empty, must
never suggest anybody has been forgotten or is lonely, and must never ask why they have not written.
It is a note about something small the writer noticed — the weather over the square, a thing that
happened at their counter, the state of the fountain. Warm and unimportant.

## The four who write

Their voices come from the character bible, which you are given. In short:

- **Nib** — the town clerk. Gruff on the surface and delighted underneath, mad about paperwork, calls
  the register "my big book". He would absolutely write a letter to make something official.
- **Stamp** — the post office. Wry. Knows everybody's post and mentions none of it. Would write a
  letter simply because a letter is a good object.
- **Moss** — the sweeper. Steady, quiet, out in all weather. Notices the square itself: the leaves,
  the light, what the night left behind.
- **Bean** — the Coffee Cup. Warm. Talks about cups, queues, the smell of the place, who came in.

## The rules

1. **A letter, not a notification.** Every one of these is in somebody's handwriting on the world's
   own paper. No system voice, no "welcome to", no announcements, no exclamation-mark greetings that
   could have come from an app.
2. **Nothing is ever owed.** No line may ask for a reply, suggest one would be nice, or imply the
   reader should do anything at all. The plan's own rule: *nothing is owed, nothing is archived.*
3. **Never name the game's parts.** No buttons, no cards, no coins, no "the address book", no
   mechanics, no numbers, no rates. The mystery rule holds here as everywhere.
4. **Short.** These sit on a torn paper slip in a card 261 pixels wide. Two or three sentences at
   most — a hundred and forty characters is the spirit of it, and the ceiling is generous rather
   than a target.
5. **They do not know what the reader has done.** These two occasions carry no facts about the
   player — not their name, not their yard, not their doings. A later slice will add letters keyed
   to things the world can verify; these two must read perfectly to a total stranger.
6. **Four distinct people.** Read the four side by side: if you could swap two signatures without
   noticing, they are not written yet.
