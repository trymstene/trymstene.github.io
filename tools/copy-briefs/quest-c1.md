# Return to Sender, chapter one — the first scene, moved to the town

Chapter one of Banana World's questline used to open at the player's own plot: Nib was waiting on
the road outside it and said, in effect, *this is Plot 11, nobody has lived here since 1999, sign
your name and it's yours.* That scene now happens **in Banana Town, by the fountain**, before the
player has ever seen the plot. So the words that were about *standing here* become words about
*a place you have to travel to* — and, as the story has always said, a place you cannot put your
name to until you have looked into something first.

Trym, 21 Sep: *"there's a plot 11, it's yours, but as the questline says you need to investigate
something more before you can end up at the plot and name it."*

This job is that one scene. The rest of chapter one is unchanged: Old Peel in the park, the tin
on the beach, the flipbook, the Rave, and the ending — which still happens **at the plot**, where
Nib meets you to write you into the book and finds a name already there, scratched out. Do not
touch the ending's words; they are not in this job.

## Where it happens, and who

The square in Banana Town. **Nib** is standing by the fountain — not at his desk, which is the
town hall. He has come out to meet the newcomer he heard was arriving. When the scene ends he walks
up to the town hall and that is where he lives from then on.

Nib is the town clerk: warm, delighted by paperwork, a storyteller, never a bureaucrat. He calls
the register *"my big book"*. He helps the Mayor. He says *"the top drawer"*, *"the yellow form"*,
never *registry*, *deed*, *archive*, *pursuant*. He heard a **rumour** that somebody new was coming
to town — that is how he knew to wait.

The only other voices are `you` (the player, one short sentence, never enthusiastic on the
player's behalf) and `paper` (the old letter, printed matter, addresses nobody).

## What the scene has to do, in order

1. Nib greets the newcomer — he heard the rumour, and here you are.
2. He explains that there is a plot with no owner in his book. **Plot 11.** Nobody has lived on it
   since 1999. There is no name on its page — only one old letter, kept in a drawer.
3. The letter is read: `paper`, the exact line below, unchanged.
4. It is yours. But — and this is the part that changed — **you cannot simply go and put your name
   on it.** The letter says *to whoever comes asking*, and asking means finding out who left it.
   Nib's book only goes back to 1999; **Old Peel, in the Park,** is older than any book. Go and ask
   him about Plot 11.
5. Nib mentions he'll be at the town hall if you need him. (He is about to walk there.)

The plot is **a destination**, never the ground under the player's feet. No line may say *here*,
*this plot*, *where you're standing*, *this land* — the player is in the square. The plot is *out
past the park*, *down the road*, *a walk from here*: somewhere to get to.

## The letter, verbatim

`paper` says exactly this and nothing else:

> “the eleventh plot, to whoever comes asking. it has waited long enough.”

It has been this since August and it is on the player's own paper in three other places. Do not
rephrase, retitle or re-punctuate it.

## Two versions of the scene

- `open` — a stranger who has never had a plot. Ten lines at most.
- `openRes` — somebody who **already lives on Plot 11** (a returning player whose homestead exists).
  Nib is not offering them land; he has come to write them in properly and found the page empty.
  Same letter, same send-off to Old Peel, shorter. Eight lines at most.

## The chip lines

- `find` — the journal chip while the scene is waiting: where to go. Lower case, very short,
  names the fountain in the town. ⚠️ it is also read in the park, the bay and the homestead as the
  compass, so it has to make sense to somebody who is not in the town yet.
- `findRes` — the same, for the resident.
- `hint` — once the scene is done: where next. Old Peel, the park. Lower case, very short.

## The rules

1. **Never say what the player gets.** No coins, no rewards, no "you'll be able to". The mystery rule.
2. **Never an instruction in a line.** Chips instruct; dialogue does not. Nib may *ask* you to go
   and see Old Peel, because that is a person talking. Nothing says "tap" or names a control.
3. **The mystery holds.** Who wrote the letter is not known and is not hinted at. Nib does not know.
4. **A 13-year-old and a 50-year-old read it without a stumble.** Short sentences, plain words,
   warmth. This is the bar chapter one was written to.
5. **The ending is not this job.** Nothing here may describe the naming, the book's scratched-out
   page, or what Nib will find. The scene ends with Old Peel and the walk to the town hall.
